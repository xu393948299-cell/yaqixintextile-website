7 Things to Check Before You Wire Money to a Chinese Fabric Supplier

Published September 5, 2026Updated September 5, 202610 min readBy YAQIXIN Editorial Team

A field-tested due diligence checklist for apparel buyers sourcing fabric from China: factory checks, sample rounds, certificate verification, payment structure and the red flags that end a conversation.

Two years ago a buyer in Lagos lost $18,400 on a 5,000-meter cotton twill order. The samples had been perfect. The bulk shipment arrived three weeks late, the GSM came in 18% under specification, and three of the eight colours were wrong. The supplier stopped answering emails the day the containers cleared customs.

That case is not unusual, and it is not the worst one we have heard. Fabric sourcing from China has grown crowded since 2020, and the hard part for buyers is no longer finding a supplier. It is telling the difference between a supplier who can deliver 5,000 consistent meters and one who disappears mid-order. This guide covers the seven checks that matter once real money is about to move. It is not a substitute for a physical sample, a lab test, or a pre-shipment inspection; it is how you decide whether a given supplier deserves those steps at all.

Why verification is worth one extra week

Verification costs days. Skipping it costs orders. A careful check of a new supplier takes roughly one working week of emails, video calls and sample logistics, and it can run in parallel with your normal sampling work. The buyers who lose the most money are rarely the ones who chose the highest price. They are the ones who skipped a step because the first conversation felt good.

Use the checklist before the first deposit

Apply the seven checks in order before approving a production sample or sending a deposit. Any single failure is a warning. Two or more, and the price is not worth it.

1. Look at the factory behind the trading company

This is the most common trap in Chinese fabric sourcing, and it costs buyers more than every other mistake combined. A trading company has an office. A factory has looms. Most “manufacturers” listed on marketplaces are trading companies: they buy from a real mill, mark the price up, and resell. Sometimes that is fine. Often it is not, because when something goes wrong with the bulk, the trading company has no leverage over the mill actually weaving your order.

Three ways to tell the difference:

  • Ask for the factory’s business licence, not just the trading company’s. Real manufacturers usually share it quickly; evasion is an answer in itself.
  • Request a live video walk-through of the production line on a specific date, with the looms running. Stock photos and pre-recorded videos prove nothing.
  • Check the export record. Customs data services list shipment history by company name. A supplier claiming fifteen years of export experience whose records show two years of activity is telling you something.

A trading-company margin is normal and reasonable. A trading company reselling fabric it never inspected is where orders die.

2. Order two rounds of samples and weigh them

Most buyers skip this. They receive a free swatch, it feels right, they wire a 30% deposit, and the bulk arrives as a different fabric. The fix is to separate approval into two rounds.

Round one: free swatches. Use these to compare colour direction and handfeel against your reference fabric, and to eliminate obvious mismatches. Remember that suppliers send their best swatches; if these do not pass, nothing will. The fabric sample inspection checklist gives you a repeatable record for this round.

Round two: paid production samples. Ask the supplier to produce one or two meters on the actual bulk-production setup, with your target colour matched to Pantone, and pay for it. A supplier who refuses a paid production sample is either hiding their production capacity or planning to substitute yarn during bulk. For weight, do not stop at touch. A 360gsm heavy cotton twill that comes back at 295gsm is a problem no amount of hand-feel will catch, and a $30 kitchen scale will catch it before a $15,000 dispute does. For the definitions behind the numbers, see the fabric GSM guide.

3. Verify certificates by number, not by logo

OEKO-TEX Standard 100, GOTS, GRS, ISO 9001: every serious fabric supplier has at least one certification, most are genuine, and some are not. Certificate misuse is common enough that checking is part of normal due diligence now, because if you label finished garments as OEKO-TEX certified on the strength of a fake fabric certificate, the compliance problem lands on you.

Two checks take ten minutes:

  • Every certification body runs a public certificate lookup. OEKO-TEX has the Check tool; GOTS and GRS have searchable databases. If the certificate number does not resolve, the certificate does not exist.
  • Ask for the certificate document itself, not the logo. The real document shows the holder’s legal name, address, product scope and expiry date. A logo on a website shows none of this.

A supplier who cannot produce a verifiable certificate number for a claimed certification still has to answer for it before you wire money, not after.

4. Put the inspection and colour clauses in the contract

Purchase contracts between overseas buyers and Chinese fabric suppliers are often three pages about price and delivery date, and silent on everything that decides a dispute. Three clauses are worth insisting on:

  • Pre-shipment inspection clause. State who pays for inspection, which standard applies (AQL 2.5 is the textile norm), and what happens if the inspection fails. Without it, a failed shipment is a negotiation rather than a procedure.
  • Colour tolerance. “Match Pantone X” means little without a tolerance. Solid colours are commonly controlled at ΔE ≤ 1.0 under D65 light; put the number and the light source in the contract.
  • Delay terms with real consequences. Force majeure clauses in China are broad. If yours lets a supplier deliver sixty days late with no remedy, your launch calendar is unprotected.

A sourcing agent or freight forwarder can review a purchase contract for a few hundred dollars. On a five-figure order, that review is cheap.

5. Structure payment so you can still walk away

Wiring a deposit to a Chinese supplier is normal. Wiring full payment to an unverified one is not. The standard structure in fabric sourcing is 30% T/T after the production sample is approved, and 70% against a third-party inspection report or a bill of lading copy. Most legitimate suppliers accept it without discussion.

Suppliers who insist on 50% or 100% upfront are usually cash-strapped or overbooked, and either way your order becomes the one that gets pushed when their schedule tightens. For a first order above roughly $30,000, a letter of credit is worth the bank fees and paperwork: it puts your bank, not your supplier’s, in control of the money. Avoid Western Union and MoneyGram entirely; there is no buyer protection on either.

6. Run a third-party inspection, even on repeat orders

Inspection companies such as SGS, Bureau Veritas and QIMA will visit the factory before shipment, pull random cartons, check defects against AQL standards and write a report that gives you leverage while the balance payment is still unpaid. A textile inspection typically costs $250 to $400 per man-day.

The instinct is to inspect new suppliers and trust old ones. The expensive cases we hear about rarely involve a new supplier. They involve a five-year relationship where the production manager who knew your specifications moved on, the night shift used a different yarn lot, and nobody inspected that shipment. One skipped inspection on a mature order cost a buyer we know roughly $90,000 in unusable finished garments. Inspect the first order, then keep inspecting.

7. Mind the cultural friction

This is not soft advice. Cultural friction quietly costs buyers efficiency on every order.

  • “Yes” does not always mean yes. A supplier saying “we can do this” often means “we will try”. If a capability is non-negotiable, ask again in writing and get confirmation from a second person at the company.
  • Relationships decide queue position. When production schedules tighten, the buyer who visits the factory gets priority over the buyer who only emails. A supplier visit every year or two is the cheapest capacity insurance available.
  • Chinese New Year is a hard stop. Factories close for two to four weeks in late January or February, and transport capacity tightens for weeks around it. If an order must ship in February, place it in November. A supplier who “promises” delivery during the holiday period is telling you what you want to hear.

Red flags that end the conversation

Price matters, but these signals matter more. We stop working with an inquiry that shows any two of them, regardless of the quote:

  • The supplier refuses to share a business licence or a live factory video.
  • They cannot name one certification body that has audited them.
  • Their quote sits 30% or more below every other quote you received.
  • They push you to skip the production sample and go straight to bulk.
  • Their export history cannot be found in customs data.
  • They require full payment before production starts.

Buyer questions

Is a trading company always a bad choice?

No. A competent trading company adds value in consolidation, communication and quality control, and many operate responsibly. The risk is not the markup; it is a middleman with no leverage over the mill when the bulk goes wrong. Verify which role your supplier actually plays, then judge their controls, not their label.

What deposit should I pay a new fabric supplier?

30% after production-sample approval and 70% against an inspection report or bill of lading copy is the market standard, and there is rarely a good reason to depart from it on a first order. Larger first orders justify a letter of credit instead of a higher deposit.

How do I check that an OEKO-TEX certificate is real?

Use the certificate lookup on the OEKO-TEX website with the certificate number from the supplier’s document. The result should show the holder’s legal name, the certified product scope and the validity period. If the supplier only offers a logo image, assume the certificate does not exist until proven otherwise.

Do I need an inspection on repeat orders?

Yes, on any order where the money would hurt. Factories change: staff turn over, yarn lots change, schedules get tight. The most expensive failures happen on mature relationships that stopped being inspected.

When should I plan around Chinese New Year?

If your order must ship in February, place it by November and confirm production scheduling in writing. Treat the two weeks either side of the holiday as congested for both production and freight, and do not accept delivery promises that fall inside the factory closure.
